Secretive Firms From U.K. to Singapore Win $200M in Tenders From Uzbek State Giant

A major Uzbek state mining company awarded hundreds of millions of dollars in tenders to companies in the U.K., Georgia, and Singapore. Reporters found they have connections to an Uzbek ping-pong official, while some have unclear ownership information or received contracts despite company filings indicating they were inactive.

↘ KEY FINDINGS

  • A state mining giant in Uzbekistan awarded more than $200 million in tenders to 10 foreign firms in multiple countries that have connections to an Uzbek ping-pong official.
  • Two of the companies made a flurry of changes to their respective corporate structures after OCCRP and Finance Uncovered inquired about their ownership.
  • Several of the companies that won tenders are, on paper at least, inactive.

Over the past three years, two U.K. companies with no prior record in the mining industry have won tens of millions of dollars in Uzbek state procurement contracts with a mining behemoth known as a “crown jewel” of Uzbekistan’s economy.

Dallas-headquartered Steward Health Care was awarded a 2.1-billion-euro ($2.5-billion) Maltese government contract in 2018 to renovate and manage three public hospitals. But when allegations of fraud and corruption threatened its cash-strapped business, Steward hired private spies at two London-based intelligence firms to deal with its opponents, the records show.

But an investigation by OCCRP has found that the two U.K. firms are part of a larger circle of companies stretching from Singapore to the South Caucasus that were awarded more than $200 million in Uzbek state contracts since 2022. The client is Almalyk Mining-Metallurgical Complex (AMMC), a state enterprise that Uzbek President Shavkat Mirziyoyev has slated for a possible initial public offering on a foreign stock exchange.

An initial public offering is the first time a company sells its shares to the public on a stock exchange. Listing abroad usually means meeting stricter disclosure rules than at home.

On the surface, these companies located thousands of kilometers apart appear to have little in common. But OCCRP found they have connections to Grigoriy Khvan, a prominent figure in the Uzbek ping-pong world, or a second man, a Colombian national with no identifiable background in the mining sector.

U.K. companies must declare their persons with significant control: anyone holding more than 25 percent of the shares or voting rights, or who otherwise controls the company.

The information is supplied by the company itself. You can look up any firm on the Companies House register.

These links raise questions about whether the individuals named in the companies’ official filings are their true owners, or whether they could be serving as proxies for someone else.

They also underscore persistent lapses in corporate transparency in the U.K., despite recent efforts by the government to tighten regulations and make it harder to create bogus companies.
